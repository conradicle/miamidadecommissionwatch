# Editorial review checklist (staff use)

Internal reference for reviewing student editorial submissions before approving them in the admin dashboard. Not published on the public site.

## Before approving

- [ ] Every factual claim about a named person or organization is either common knowledge, sourced by the student, or verifiable against public record (agenda item, meeting minutes, news coverage). If a claim can't be traced to something, ask the student for a source or ask them to cut it.
- [ ] Opinions read as opinions ("I think," "this seems," "in my view") rather than being phrased as settled fact. An editorial can argue a position; it shouldn't assert disputed facts as certain.
- [ ] No claims about a named person's motives, character, or private conduct that go beyond what the record supports. Criticizing a vote or a policy is different from asserting something about the person that isn't documented.
- [ ] The submitter's grade is 9th–12th (the submission form only accepts these, but double-check nothing looks off).

## When editing a submission

- Edit for length, clarity, grammar, and readability freely.
- Don't rewrite or add to the student's substantive factual claims yourself. If a claim looks legally risky or unsupported, send it back and ask the student to revise or cut it, rather than fixing it on their behalf. This keeps the published piece clearly the student's own speech, not staff's.
- If in doubt about whether an edit crosses that line, ask the other founder before publishing.

## If something's already live and turns out to be wrong

Follow the site's public corrections policy (see about.html): fix it promptly, and if the error was significant, consider a brief note on the piece. Keep a record of what changed and when, in case it's ever asked about later.

## Why this exists

Commissioners and the Mayor are public officials, which raises the bar (the "actual malice" standard) for any defamation claim against factual reporting about their public conduct. That protection is much less certain for editorials, which are explicitly opinion content, about identifiable people, written by students who aren't professional journalists. This checklist is the cheap, five-minute version of due diligence that keeps that risk low without slowing down publishing.
