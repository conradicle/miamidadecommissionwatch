On October 20, item 11A2 was proposed with the goal of helping the primary thing that keeps free-market capitalism running: our small businesses. The U.S. is often considered to be where anyone has the chance to be anything, but this is not possible without legislative aid. Therefore, this resolution makes the American dream possible by giving small businesses a platform to voice their concerns and complaints as they navigate confusing and burdensome federal regulations. The DUMP (Destroying Unnecessary, Misaligned and Prohibitive) Red Tape Act is a legislative proposal that calls for a hotline “to receive a notification from a small entity relating to the burden of complying with a rule, guidance, policy statement, or other activity of an agency that is applicable to such concern” as stated by the Congressional committee of Small Businesses and Entrepreneurship. Federal regulations must always be followed but may not always be understood. By implementing this act, small businesses can conduct themselves properly according to regulations by being able to reach out for help in doing so. Commissioners have been asked to urge Congress to pass the DUMP Red Tape Act; and just as this board has the power to persuade the federal government, this act gives our local businesses the power to communicate with them too. Along with a hotline, this act mandates for reports to be made detailing which rules are most often and most likely to cause problems for businesses. These reports allow congress to listen to the people and find solutions that make rules easier for small businesses to follow. Therefore, the board urging for this act to be passed means protecting the power of the people’s voice in changing how government regulates us. This proposal is not precautionary but rather is trying to solve problems that are already occurring. NBC 6 has reported a prime example, where a driving school in Miami-Dade was suddenly shut down despite belief regulations had been followed. Owner Eli Soler received a letter that several instructors had “failed to meet the federal English proficiency requirement during subsequent roadside inspections.” Meanwhile, Soler had previously received a letter that led him to believe that all regulations had been addressed. This resolution stops other businesses like Soler & Soler from being unable to recognize and address regulations that may have been otherwise unnoticed before it is too late. With this proposal, the commission board has the opportunity to protect small businesses not just in Miami-Dade, but in the whole country. This act is necessary for the greater good of our open markets. After all, what are we without our small businesses, the center of the American Dream?

Miami-Dade County has one simple problem that keeps getting more complicated. We keep hearing about responsible growth, but then we make exceptions when development comes along that officials want to approve. That is why I believe our County Commission made the wrong decision when it voted in September to allow Kelly Tractor to move forward with a project outside the county’s Urban Development Boundary. This vote was 9 to 3, which was enough to override Mayor Daniella Levine Cava’s veto. The Urban Development Boundary is supposed to help Miami-Dade decide where urban development belongs as opposed to where it does not. In February of 2026, Mayor Levine Cava vetoed the Kelly Tractor land-use change because it would have allowed development outside the boundary on land involving wetlands. Her office then released a statement, which said that the proposal affected approximately 160 acres of wetlands and did not contain enough enforceable protections for those areas. I understand why commissioners might support a project like this. Development is crucial for regions, its a key economic necessity, from areas such as creating new jobs to expanding businesses. But that does not mean every project is worth changing the rules that are supposed to guide development. What worries our community, and me, is the precedent this infringes upon. If the Urban Development Boundary can be pushed aside whenever a particular project seems economically useful, then it stops functioning as a meaningful boundary. The county has already taken other steps in this direction: in July, commissioners approved a resolution directing the administration to prepare a Comprehensive Development Master Plan amendment addressing text amendments that could facilitate urban land uses outside the boundary. Whilst that doesn't automatically mean every future project outside the boundary will be approved, it does mean Miami-Dade is creating a decision about making development beyond the existing line easier. And that conversation deserves more attention from residents, especially young people who will live with the consequences of today’s decisions. It is true that growth is needed, the increasing population needs housing, jobs, transportation and businesses. But this growth has to happen orderly and in specific areas, and where it happens matters. Wetlands and other environmentally sensitive areas are not empty spaces waiting for someone to build on them. The mayor’s office pointed out that wetlands provide water management, habitat to various species, and flood protection benefits. The Commission’s decision on Kelly Tractor does not mean Miami-Dade will suddenly be covered in buildings outside the boundary, however government policies are built one exception at a time. If officials continue treating the boundary as something that can be moved or worked around whenever a project is attractive enough, residents should question whether the boundary means what it says.The county should not have to choose between economic growth and environmental responsibility. It should demand both. Miami-Dade’s government have the current power to decide what the county will look like decades from now, and they should use that power carefully. A boundary designed to protect responsible growth should be treated as a real limit rather than merely an obstacle that can be overcome when the right project comes along.

On September 1, commissioners in Miami-Dade County voted 9-3 to allow Kelly Tractor, a heavy-equipment company, to develop on 140-plus acres of environmentally fragile wetlands, outside the Urban Development Boundary, an area designated to protect wetlands and agricultural lands from sprawling development, located near Northwest 137th Avenue and the Dolphin Expressway in west Miami-Dade County. The development was already deferred for the eighth time. Commissioner Danielle Cohen Higgins, one of the three dissenters, described her colleagues as voting to get to the necessary number. While the issue here is clearly the wetlands, what really matters about the vote is the way that this approval got to the commissioners in the first place: through the process of "text amendment," a procedure designed for technical changes to county code, not the decision of whether a company should be allowed to build outside of the boundary designed to contain growth within the county. A request for moving the boundary would go through a much more stringent review process than a text amendment does. Kelly Tractor's owner claims that the county's planning department directed him to use this loophole three years ago. Here's the primary issue, and why I believe people should care about how this affects our county: Two months prior to this vote, the very same commission unanimously voted legislation to close this loophole to future developers. Raquel Regalado, the commissioner who favored the closure and voted yes on Kelly Tractor, thought the solution was a necessity. A necessity moving forward, seemingly, but this vote didn’t reflect that. They voted to close the loophole, then let one more project through it before the closure takes effect. The justification for the yes votes isn't without merit. Kelly Tractor agreed to protect and fund restoration of over 80 acres of wetlands indefinitely and even gave land to the county in the past. Deputy Mayor Roy Coley said the firm showed economic need, labeling it as an international competitor in the machinery industry as well as an emergency assistance provider to all of South Florida. Coley also claimed that the mayor's office made sure the applicant met almost the same criteria as a boundary expansion application would have required. But a legitimate outcome does not justify a flawed process, as the outcome is totally dependent on which company shows up at the hearing and the strength of its legal representation. The next applicant does won't necessarily offer an additional eighty acres worth of preservation funding. As Cohen Higgins points out, this approval provides an incentive for the rest of the development community to do the same thing before the new rule is enacted. If the commissioners had already decided, by unanimous vote, that such a loophole should not exist going forward, then they should not have done a one last dance sort of move before the door closed.

When concerns are raised about the length of this program, I will remind you of the implementation of the sunset program. The USF may have been implemented over forty-years ago, but it still finds its way to positively impact Miami-Dade.

In 2004, voters of Miami-Dade County approved of the construction of a recovery facility meant to treat people with serious mental health illnesses rather than putting them into traditional county jails. This building was finally finished in 2004, yet it remained desolate until 2026, being fully built but the county had not approved the funds and agreements needed to operate it. However, after 22 years, commissioners this June have finally approved for funding to allow the center to open. 22 years is not a governmental hiccup, it's a full generation. People who were children when voters first said yes to this project are now adults who may have cycled through the very jail system the center was designed to divert them from. That gap should unsettle anyone who assumes that once a policy is "approved," it's actually happening. We can take away that this instance here is not really about mental health funding specifically, but it's about what happens between a government's promise and its actions it takes to follow through with that problem. A referendum passing, a commission voting yes, even a building getting constructed: none of that guarantees implementation. Somewhere between approval and execution, resolutions get stuck in legal review, priorities shift, and appointed officials come and go. The center only moved forward now because of sustained pressure from advocates as well as media pressure kept the issue in the county’s sight. And this matters far beyond this one case, rules for artificial intelligence are beginning to be rolled out. Cities and states are beginning to pass AI oversight commissions, algorithmic accountability boards, and "responsible AI" resolutions; often with fanfare and unanimous votes. But a commission created on paper is not the same as a commission that's funded, staffed, and actually reviewing systems. If a mental health center that voters approved by referendum could stall for two decades, there's little reason to assume AI governance bodies, usually created with far less public pressure behind them, will fare better once the press conference ends. The practical takeaway for those who care about how AI gets regulated is that the passage is not the finish. The real work is in tracking what happens after the vote, whether the funding shows up, whether the staff gets hired, and whether anyone keeps asking hard questions. Miami-Dade's mental health center finally opened because people refused to let it be forgotten. Future AI legislation will need the same kind of persistence, or it risks becoming another building with the lights off.

Drive down almost any residential street in Miami Dade County and you will find the same thing: cracked asphalt, sunken patches, and potholes deep enough to rattle a car’s suspension or blow out a tire. From the side streets of Little Havana to the corridors of downtown Miami, deteriorating roads have become a routine hazard for drivers, cyclists, and pedestrians alike. The County has taken steps to address the problem, but those steps have not kept pace with how widespread and persistent it has become. Miami Dade’s Department of Transportation and Public Works runs a program called “Potholepalooza,” which rotates through commission districts, asking residents to report potholes so crews can patch them, generally within about a week of a report. Since it launched in 2023, the program has repaired well over a thousand potholes. That is real progress, and it reflects a model worth praising: a government that responds directly to the people who use the roads every day. But the program’s own structure reveals its limits. It moves district by district, meaning a resident’s street might get attention for a two or three month window and then wait years before crews return. Patches applied during that window often do not last, particularly once South Florida’s rainy season arrives and water works its way back into the same weak spots. Residents in the Redland area have raised exactly this concern, circulating a petition for the County to commit to dedicated, ongoing funding for road resurfacing rather than relying on a rotating pilot program to catch problems as they surface. Downtown Miami adds its own layer to this problem. Heavy truck and bus traffic, combined with near constant construction, puts more stress on pavement than a typical suburban street ever sees. A pothole downtown is not just a nuisance drivers swerve around, it is a hazard for cyclists sharing narrow lanes with cars and for pedestrians navigating crosswalks pockmarked by broken asphalt. As the County works to expand bike lanes and micromobility infrastructure, as covered elsewhere on this site, that effort will only matter if the pavement underneath is actually safe to ride on. The deeper issue is funding structure, not effort. Reactive, resident reported repair is useful, but it treats potholes as isolated incidents rather than as symptoms of aging infrastructure that needs systematic resurfacing. A road that gets patched five times in three years costs the County more, in labor and materials, than one that gets properly resurfaced once. It also costs residents more, in the form of vehicle damage, insurance claims, and, in the worst cases, accidents. Miami Dade County should build on Potholepalooza rather than treat it as the finished solution. That means dedicating consistent annual funding to road resurfacing, not just pothole patching, so that repairs address root causes instead of visible symptoms. It means tracking which roads get patched repeatedly and prioritizing them for full resurfacing rather than another temporary fix. And it means being transparent with residents about how long they can expect to wait for their street to see real repair, not just another rotation of the pilot program. None of this is about criticizing the crews doing the patching or the residents reporting the problems. Both are doing exactly what the current system asks of them. The responsibility lies with the Commission to fund infrastructure maintenance at a scale that matches the size and age of Miami Dade’s road network. Reporting a pothole should not be a resident’s only recourse for keeping the street in front of their home safe to drive, bike, or walk on.

Every year, Military Veterans return from their line of work after an exhaustive service to their Country. A future exploration and application of amending job descriptions to allow military work experience to substitute college degrees could significantly open certain job industries in technology or other fields. But just finding the right jobs isn’t enough. Miami-Dade County must also have a transition plan for Veterans to get into the Workforce again, so this would need a strong program with proper infrastructure, resources, and budget allotted to provide a program to help Veterans prepare themselves for civilian jobs. Think of it like a college class: Miami-Dade takes Veterans, prepares them for a job they’ve identified using proper soft skills and job training, and sends them out as capable and ready Co-workers. Will it be an easy feat? I don’t believe so, but if it was, then this issue wouldn’t be in our attention today. If Miami-Dade County wants to provide an opportunity for Veterans, then they need to be ready to help them at every step of the way. Afterall, if Veterans put their heart and soul into helping keep the country safe, it only makes sense that we welcome them back in with open arms, and don’t close the door on Veterans anymore.

Two long-delayed decisions by the Miami-Dade County Commission highlight a fundamental truth of local governance: putting off necessary decisions does not save money, it just makes the eventual solutions harder and more expensive. 1. Mental Health Over Incarceration The 20-year saga of the Miami Center for Mental Health and Recovery, Conceived by retired Judge Steven Leifman and approved by voters in 2004, illustrates the flaw of constructing infrastructure without securing operational governance. Though physically completed in 2024, the facility sat vacant during prolonged debates over service frameworks and long-term liabilities. As detailed by WLRN Public Media, Commission Chairman Anthony Rodriguez brokered the final unanimous vote by incorporating key policy additions: an outpatient tracking center, dual-diagnosis substance abuse treatment, and independent oversight by Jackson Health System. Awarding a $31 million contract to non-profits WestCare Foundation and The Advocate Program, which was funded partly through Florida's state opioid litigation settlements alongside $14 million in operating costs, establishes a sustainable model to divert chronically incarcerated individuals into healthcare rather than penal facilities. 2. Infrastructure Sovereignty Relationship With Real Estate Capital The conflict surrounding PortMiami’s fuel supply highlights a failure in municipal land-use foresight. Mayor Daniella Levine Cava’s administration initiated eminent domain backed by Seaport Revenue Bonds rather than the general fund, triggering severe administrative friction that contributed to the resignations of multiple top seaport directors. The impending courtroom struggle centers on property valuation, where estimates range from $25 million (reflecting a restrictive land covenant limiting the parcel strictly to fuel storage) to $430 million. A Lane in the Right Direction

As electric bicycles, scooters, and other micromobility devices become increasingly common across Miami-Dade County, local regulations need to keep pace. At its July 21 meeting, the Miami-Dade County Board of County Commissioners considered an ordinance establishing regulations for the operation of micromobility devices, electric bicycles, and motorized scooters on public rights-of-way throughout incorporated and unincorporated areas of the County. I support the County’s decision to establish clearer rules for these devices. E-bikes and scooters can provide residents with convenient alternatives to cars, especially for shorter trips, however, their growing popularity has also created conflicts between riders, pedestrians, and drivers. Clear and enforceable regulations are necessary to ensure that micromobility remains a useful transportation option without creating unnecessary risks for others. Still, I believe the County should go further, particularly regarding where these devices can be operated. Where safe and accessible bicycle lanes exist, e-bikes and motorized scooters should generally be required to use them rather than sidewalks. As a resident of Miami Beach, I've witnessed many accidents occur because someone was recklessly riding their motorized scooter on the sidewalk, or because they did not follow traffic laws and caused harm to themselves. Sidewalks are primarily designed for pedestrians, and the difference in speed between someone walking and someone traveling on an electric bicycle or scooter can create dangerous situations. This is particularly concerning in dense and heavily walked areas of Miami-Dade. However, such a requirement would need to be paired with better infrastructure. Many streets throughout the County lack protected or continuous bike lanes, and forcing riders into automobile traffic without providing a safe alternative could create an even greater danger. Miami-Dade should therefore continue expanding protected bike lanes and infrastructure, like they have on Park Avenue and Ocean Drive, that can safely accommodate both traditional bicycles and newer micromobility devices The goal should not be to discourage e-bikes or scooters. They can be valuable parts of a transportation system that gives residents alternatives to driving. Instead, the County should create an environment where pedestrians, cyclists, drivers, and micromobility users can coexist safely. The ordinance considered by the Commission is a positive step, but it should be viewed as a foundation. As micromobility continues to grow, Miami-Dade should continue adapting its regulations and infrastructure to make these new forms of transportation safer for everyone.
